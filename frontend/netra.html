Find the wallets worth investigating — and show the evidence.

Bitcoin transactions are public, but they are not named. NETRA reads a bulk dump of network traffic and transaction records, works out which wallets are controlled by the same person, works out which IP addresses controlled them, and then ranks the ones that behave like money laundering — with the reason attached to each one.

  1. 1
    Read the traffic. Any of CSV, JSON, XML or a SQLite database. Rows that cannot be parsed are reported, never quietly dropped.
  2. 2
    Group wallets into people. In Bitcoin you must sign for every wallet you spend from, so wallets spent together belong to one owner.
  3. 3
    Join the two worlds. We match the IP that broadcast a transaction with the wallet it moved — that is how a wallet gets a country.
  4. 4
    Rank, and explain. Two trained models score every group, and each score comes with the factors that produced it and the money trail it follows.

What you are about to see

The console has four panels. Each one answers a different question an investigator actually asks, in the order they ask it.

What changed? A live feed of events since the last batch — a wallet that got riskier, one that went quiet, one that suddenly gained addresses.
How does it fit together? A map of money moving between wallet groups, with dashed links showing which IP addresses were controlling them.
Why this one? The evidence for the selected lead: which factors pushed the score up, what pushed it down, and where the money went afterwards.
Is the data sound? How much traffic was read, what was rejected and why, and whether this batch looks like the data the models were trained on.

Everything here runs on this machine. No internet connection is used or needed — that is a requirement of the problem statement, not a preference.
